Data governance01

One source of truth, with a reason behind every change

The catalogue was one spreadsheet that four teams edited at the same time. Someone would paste in an incomplete file over a complete one, data would disappear without anyone noticing, and we would find out weeks later when a salesperson quoted a price that no longer existed.

When a number looked wrong, no one could tell who changed it or when. The problem was never the data. It was that there were no rules for editing it.

  • 01Editing the master file directly is now switched off. Everything goes through import, submit, approve, archive — and nothing gets in without someone approving the change.
  • 02Updates happen field by field. The system copies the current row, changes only the fields you selected, and leaves everything else untouched. That is what stops an empty cell from wiping out good data.
  • 03The pricing team's columns are locked, so an approval from the product side cannot overwrite what another team owns.
  • 04The change log sits in the same file as the data. Keeping history in a separate file means syncing two files, and when syncing fails there is no warning.

Supplier tracking02

Catching what suppliers change without telling us

Factories change minimum order quantities, discontinue products and adjust costs without announcing it. Answering “what changed since last quarter” meant opening old files and comparing by eye, so in the end nobody asked.

The approval log alone could never answer this. It only records changes that went through review, so anyone editing the file directly leaves no trace — and those are the changes you most want to catch.

  • 01A scheduled job saves a snapshot of every tracked field once a day, so there is always a record — approved or not.
  • 02The comparison is built around the question people actually ask: this factory, this period, what moved.
  • 03Product codes are cleaned on the way in. A single trailing space used to split one product into two, and neither version looked wrong.

Catalogue analysis03

The zero that actually meant<br>“no price yet”

No one could say which design and size combinations we actually sell, or which ones have stock but have never been priced. My first version of this view was wrong twice, and both mistakes came from reading the data without understanding the business behind it.

A price of zero does not mean the tile is cheap. It means the pricing team has not got to it yet. Without filtering those out, every price range in the report came out as 0 baht.

  • 01Count items and SKUs separately. Many rows share the same group code, so counting rows makes the range look much wider than it really is. Try the switch below the diagram.
  • 02Zero prices are kept out of every price range, and cells with stock but no price are highlighted so they stop slipping past.
  • 03The import matches columns by their header name, not their position, and takes a whole file pasted in — because whoever sends it almost never trims it first.

Internal documents04

Getting rid of the paper forms

Memos, sample requests and expense claims were all printed forms, filled in by hand, signed, scanned and then lost. Finding last month's request meant asking the person who wrote it.

The form itself was the easy part. The hard part was making any amount of text fit one A4 page in two languages without anyone having to think about the layout.

  • 01The page resizes itself to fit A4 no matter how much is typed, so a long request never quietly runs onto a second page.
  • 02Thai and English switch together across the whole system, including what comes out of the printer.
  • 03Sending opens an email draft with the PDF already attached, because attaching the file was the step people kept forgetting.

Working method

How I work

I use AI to help me build these, which is a fair thing to ask about. Here is what it actually looks like.

01

Go and watch the work first

What people ask for is usually just a symptom. “Can you add a column” often means something upstream is broken. I watch someone do the job before I suggest anything.

02

Write the spec myself

What goes in, what comes out, who owns which field, what happens when two people edit at once, what the system does with a value it does not recognise. This is what decides whether it works, and no tool writes it for me.

03

Build fast, then try to break it

I test with the files that really turn up: columns in the wrong order, three title rows above the header, codes with extra spaces, a zero that actually means missing. This is where most of my time goes.

04

Release small, stay close

One team, one workflow, then watch how it gets used. Every system here changed after the first week, because people always use things in ways nobody wrote down.

AI cuts development time from months to days, but it is the human who understands the context, defines the problem, and determines the most practical solution for the business.
